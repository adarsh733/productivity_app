# Voice & speech profile — the measurements

**Assessed:** 2026-08-12 (self-administered) · **Subject:** Adarsh
**Source artifact:** [`Voice_Profile_and_Training_Plan.pdf`](Voice_Profile_and_Training_Plan.pdf) —
this file is the searchable transcription of it. The PDF is the original; where
they differ, the PDF wins.

This document holds **facts, not plans**. It is the baseline every future retest
compares against, and it is the evidence base for [`PLAN.md`](PLAN.md) §1.
Nothing here should be edited except to append a new retest.

> **The one-line summary.** There is nothing wrong with the voice. Vocal folds
> seal properly, breath capacity is normal, endurance is normal. What exists is
> a **habit** — speaking at roughly twice the air pressure the job requires, all
> day, every day. Every symptom is downstream of that one habit.

### The contradiction, and why it was the key

Three good results and one bad one, same lungs, same folds, same day. Tissue
damage does not behave that way — damage shows up in *every* task. So: what was
different about the task that failed?

**It was the only task with no back-pressure.**

- **Counting** has consonants and micro-stops built in; every plosive briefly
  interrupts the airstream. The task self-regulates output.
- **/z/** is *semi-occluded* — the constriction at the teeth creates pressure
  above the folds and props them into efficient, low-effort vibration. This is
  the exact mechanism behind straw phonation therapy. A therapeutic exercise was
  accidentally performed, and scored well.
- **Open "aaah"** has nothing restraining the airflow. Nothing holds you back,
  and the moment nothing holds you back, you dump air.

When something restricts airflow → efficient. When the road opens → floor it.
**That is a drive problem, not a capacity problem.**

## 2. Diagnosis

> **ROOT CAUSE — chronic excess subglottic pressure.** A habitual over-drive of
> the voice. Habitual "comfortable" volume is other people's loud. A learned
> motor habit; not a condition, not a defect, not damage.

**The causal chain:** habitual over-loud speaking → excess pressure below the
folds → pressed phonation, larynx rides up, jaw and tongue tighten → air dumped
fast + harsh high-frequency energy + shortened vocal tract → short breath, harsh
tone, merged words, no soft gear, fatigue.

| Symptom | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Running out of breath | Air spent at ~1.6× the necessary rate. The tank is not small; the throttle is open. Confirmed: 15 s loud vs 25 s soft. |
| Words merging, endings vanishing | **Acoustic masking.** Loud energy persists briefly and drowns whatever follows. The quietest, shortest sounds in speech are exactly the word-final consonants (t, d, k, p, s) — and those consonants *are* the boundaries between words. A louder voice needs *more* space around each word, not less. This is why "slow down" is not generic advice here — it compensates for a specific acoustic property of this voice. |
| Harsh tone | Pressed phonation generates *more* high-frequency energy. Harshness is a by-product of pressure, not of pitch or personality. |
| Loss of perceived depth | Under high drive the larynx climbs, which *shortens* the vocal tract and *raises* resonance. The pushing makes the voice sound **less** deep while costing effort — paying for the opposite of what was wanted. |
| No quiet register | A skill never practised, because it was never needed. See §4. |
| Only one emphasis dial | If loudness is the only expressive variable, every emphasis raises the average volume, which reinforces the habit. Pitch, pace and pause are the missing dials. |

### Ruled out — write this down, it will be doubted again in a year

| Possible cause | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fold closure problem (nodules, polyps, cyst, paresis, glottal gap) | **Ruled out** | s/z 0.72, far below the 1.4 threshold |
| Insufficient breath support / lung capacity | **Ruled out** | Count of 28, within normal range |
| Poor vocal endurance | **Ruled out** | /s/ 18 s and /z/ 25 s, both normal |
| Anatomically limited pitch | **Not a defect** | F0 is set by fold length and mass; essentially fixed in an adult man. Normal anatomy, not a problem to solve |
| Habitual over-drive | **Confirmed** | 15 s → 25 s on a single changed variable; semi-occluded /z/ outscoring the open vowel |
| Untrained prosody / tonal control | **Confirmed** | Anger projects, softness does not. No history of deliberate practice |

**Note on the straw-in-water test:** its timing is *not* comparable to open MPT —
part of the air budget maintains bubble pressure. Use the straw as an exercise,
never as a measurement.

## 3. Two perception traps

**Trap 1 — bone conduction.** You hear yourself through air *and* through your
skull; bone conduction adds low-frequency energy no listener receives. Your voice
sounds thicker and bassier to you than to the room. A recording will sound
thinner and sharper than you remember — **the recording is the true one.** Do not
tune the voice by internal feel.

**Trap 2 — "heavy" ≠ "deep".** Two independent dials, and conflating them was the
core confusion in the original question.

| | Pitch (F0) | Resonance |
|---|---|---|
| Determined by | Length and mass of the vocal folds | Shape of throat, mouth, pharynx |
| Changeable? | Barely — it is anatomy | Substantially — it is posture |
| Gives you | The note | Perceived richness, warmth, depth |
| Your lever? | **No** | **Yes — all the gains are here** |

Can the voice get deeper? **Pitch: no**, not meaningfully — anyone selling a
pitch-lowering method is selling vocal fry. **Perceived depth: yes**,
substantially. What was being felt as heaviness was most likely *loudness plus
tension*, not low-end richness.

## 4. Sub-problem A — the missing quiet register

Soft speech is not "the same thing turned down". It is mechanically a different
task. Loud speech is high pressure plus firm closure — momentum does the work and
forgives sloppy coordination. Soft speech is low pressure plus *precisely* the
right closure: too little and the tone breaks into breathiness, too much and it
refuses to start. There is no momentum to hide behind.

Years of training only the loud coordination means the quiet one has never been
practised. **This is the most trainable item in the whole document** — pure skill
acquisition from zero, no anatomical obstacle. Expect noticeable change in 3–4
weeks of daily practice.

### The volume ladder

| Level | Label | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Near-whisper, but **voiced** | Silent room, person beside you. Must still have tone — a whisper is not level 1. The hardest level, and the missing one |
| 2 | Quiet conversational | Two people, quiet room. **The realistic everyday target** |
| 3 | Normal conversational | Small group, ordinary room |
| 4 | Room-filling | Meeting, presentation, 10+ people. **Probably where the current default sits** |
| 5 | Projection | Noisy environment, large room. Should be rare and deliberate |

Current everyday setting is roughly a **4**. The goal is to make **2** the default
and **1** available on demand. Levels 4 and 5 are not taken away — they become
choices instead of the only option.

## 5. Sub-problem B — harsh instead of polite

Two layers. **Layer 1, acoustic (~40%):** pressed phonation adds high-frequency
energy, and hard onsets read as abrupt. This improves automatically as the volume
habit fades. **Layer 2, prosodic (~60%):** politeness is not a quality of the
voice, it is a specific learnable pattern of pitch, pace and pause. Anger *is* in
the repertoire because anger's pattern (loud, fast, hard onsets, flat-then-spiking
pitch) is very close to the default. That asymmetry is fully explained by this.

### The six parameters of polite speech

| Parameter | Harsh setting | Polite setting |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hard glottal attack — the word starts with a click | Soft onset — a breath of air fractionally before the tone. Practise with a barely-audible "h", then remove the h and keep the feel |
| Terminal pitch | Flat or clipped endings, abrupt stops | Gentle falling contour on statements — reads as calm and settled |
| Pitch variability | Narrow range — reads flat, cold, impatient | Moderate movement across the phrase. Warmth lives in melody, not volume |
| Pace | Fast, especially at sentence start | Slower onset; **the first three words set the perceived attitude of the whole utterance** |
| Pause | Emphasis by getting louder | **Emphasis by pausing *before* the important word. The single highest-leverage swap available** — it replaces the only emphasis dial with a better one and lowers average volume at the same time |
| Volume floor | Sits at level 4 | Sits at level 2. Softness itself reads as consideration for the listener |

### The emotional palette

One neutral sentence — e.g. *"I'll look at it tomorrow morning."* — delivered in
six modes, recorded in one take for back-to-back comparison:

1. **Neutral / informational** — the baseline.
2. **Warm / friendly** — soft onsets, more pitch movement, slower start.
3. **Reassuring** — lower volume, slower, falling terminals, longer pauses.
4. **Firm but polite** — the hardest and most useful. Steady pitch, deliberate
   pace, **no volume increase at all**. The register most professional situations
   actually require.
5. **Apologetic / softening** — quietest, slowest, highest pitch variation.
6. **Enthusiastic** — fast, high variation, explicitly *without* raising volume.
   Directly attacks the loudness-equals-energy reflex.

Early on several will sound nearly identical. **That is the finding, not a
failure.** Progress = a listener could identify each one blind.

## 6. The daily routine (12 minutes)

Ordered deliberately. Release before production; the **transfer moment** at the
end of each block is where the learning happens. Straw work is step one, not step
two — the /z/ result already proved semi-occluded work is effective here.

### Block A — Release (2 min)
| Exercise | Dose | What "correct" feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Yawn-sigh | 6 reps | Begin a real yawn; feel the larynx drop and the throat open into a wide, cool space. Before it completes, sigh out on "haaah" holding that open feeling. **That dropped position is the target posture for everything else** |
| Jaw release | 30 s | Jaw hangs loose and heavy. Massage the masseter. A clamped jaw at speed is the direct cause of consonant collapse |
| Tongue forward | 30 s | Tip behind the lower front teeth, body low and forward, not bunched back toward the throat |
| Neck & larynx check | 15 s | Fingers on the front of the throat, speak a short sentence. If the larynx rides up, that is the habit showing itself. Only notice it here — do not correct |

### Block B — Semi-occluded work / SOVT (3 min)
| Exercise | Dose | What "correct" feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Straw phonation | 2 min | Steady hum through a narrow straw, then glide slowly through the range. Should feel almost effortless, buzz in the lips and face. If it feels like work, you are still pushing |
| Straw into water | 30 s | ~2 inches of water. Bubbles must stay **steady and even** — evenness is the objective, not duration |
| **The transfer moment** | 30 s | **The most important 30 seconds of the routine.** Remove the straw and immediately speak one ordinary sentence keeping the exact same easy feeling. The straw is not the exercise — carrying its feeling into speech is the exercise. **Nothing here matters if this step is skipped** |

### Block C — The volume ladder (3 min)
| Exercise | Dose | What "correct" feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Descend the ladder | 5 reps | Same sentence at 5→4→3→2→1, each step a distinct deliberate change, not a fade. Then reverse |
| Level 1 hold | 1 min | Continuous level 1 — genuinely quiet but **voiced**, never whispered. When it breaks up, stop, reset, restart. Those break points are the coordination being built |
| Silent-room simulation | 30 s | In a genuinely quiet room, 3–4 sentences at level 1–2 as though to someone sitting beside you |
| Volume anchoring | 30 s | Find level 2 and hold it for four sentences without drifting up. Drift is the default and will happen constantly. **Noticing the drift is the skill** |

### Block D — Resonance and depth (2 min)
| Exercise | Dose | What "correct" feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Chest hum | 1 min | Hand flat on the sternum; hum low and find the pitch where vibration under the palm is strongest — usually the lower third of the range. That is the resonant sweet spot |
| Hum-to-speech | 30 s | Slide from that hum directly into a sentence, keeping the chest buzz alive through the words. **This is where perceived depth comes from** |
| Open-throat sentence | 30 s | Speak holding the yawn-sigh posture. Fingers on the throat to verify the larynx is not climbing |

### Block E — Prosody and tone (2 min)
| Exercise | Dose | What "correct" feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Pause-for-emphasis | 1 min | One key word, emphasised **only** by pausing before it. Volume stays completely flat. Repeat with a different key word |
| Soft onsets | 30 s | Start vowel-initial words with a faint "h" ahead of the tone, then remove the h keeping the airy beginning. The main acoustic ingredient of politeness |
| Emotional palette | 30 s | One sentence, two of the six modes, rotating daily. Record and compare |

### All-day background habits
- **Pause instead of amplifying.** Every urge to get louder for emphasis → insert
  a beat of silence instead.
- **Space between words.** This voice needs more room around each word than most
  people's, because of masking. Speed costs intelligibility at a rate it does not
  cost others.
- **Falling endings.** Land statements downward; do not trail off into a clipped
  stop.
- **Hydration.** Genuinely improves fold pliability. No trick beyond it.
- **Larynx spot-checks.** Two fingers on the throat mid-sentence, a few times a
  day. Riding up = the old habit has returned.

## 8. Things that would make this worse

- **Do not push pitch downward** below the natural range. The single most common
  self-inflicted voice injury: vocal fry, fast fatigue, sounds forced to everyone
  except you, and over time can genuinely irritate the folds. Depth comes from
  **opening up**, never from **pressing down**.
- **Do not whisper** as a substitute for speaking softly. Whispering is more
  strain, not less. Level 1 must be voiced.
- **Do not use volume for emphasis** while trying to lower average volume. The
  two goals are in direct conflict. Pause instead.
- **Do not treat straw phonation as the goal.** Without the transfer rep it is a
  pleasant ritual that changes nothing.
- **Do not judge the voice by internal feel.** Bone conduction lies. Record.
- **Do not chase throat-clearing.** It is a percussive impact on the folds —
  swallow or sip water instead.
- **Do not push through hoarseness.** Semi-occluded work and rest, not more reps.

## 9. When to actually see a doctor

Nothing in the measurements suggests a medical problem, and this is a training
reference rather than a medical evaluation. These specific signals warrant an ENT
or a speech-language pathologist rather than more self-directed practice:

- Hoarseness persisting more than 2–3 weeks without an obvious cause such as a cold.
- The **s/z ratio crossing 1.4** on a retest — that would indicate a closure
  problem that was not present in August 2026.
- Pain while speaking, as distinct from effort or fatigue.
- Sudden loss of range, or a voice that cuts out without warning.
- Difficulty swallowing, or a persistent sensation of a lump in the throat.
- Coughing up blood, or unexplained persistent throat pain.
- Doing the routine consistently for 8–12 weeks with MPT refusing to move at all.

An SLP is also simply the fastest route even without red flags — one or two
sessions of direct feedback would compress months of self-guided trial and error,
particularly for the quiet-register work where the feedback loop is hardest to
run alone.

## 10. Appendix — reproducible test protocol

Run these identically each time, or comparison to the August 2026 baseline is
meaningless.

1. **Conditions:** quiet room, seated upright, at least two hours after waking,
   not immediately after a long stretch of talking. Hydrated. Same time of day
   where possible.
2. **Count on one breath:** one *comfortable* breath — not a maximal one — count
   aloud at conversational pace until you must breathe. Record the final number.
3. **Sustained /s/:** comfortable breath, hold a steady "sssss" as long as
   possible. Time it. Rest 30 s.
4. **Sustained /z/:** same with "zzzzz". Time it. Rest 30 s.
5. **s/z ratio:** /s/ seconds ÷ /z/ seconds. **Record both raw numbers as well as
   the ratio** — the ratio alone hides whether both values were healthy or both
   were short.
6. **MPT, habitual volume:** comfortable breath, sustain an open "aaah" at normal
   speaking volume as steadily as possible. **Do not push for a record** — the
   point is to measure the habit as it actually is.
7. **MPT, soft volume:** the same, at a volume that feels almost uncomfortably
   soft. Take the best of three attempts for each MPT measure.

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Subglottic pressure** | Air pressure below the vocal folds. More pressure = more volume and more air spent. The habitual level here is too high — the root cause of everything in this document |
| **MPT** | Maximum Phonation Time — how long a vowel can be sustained on one breath. Here it measures vocal **efficiency**, not lung capacity |
| **s/z ratio** | Sustained /s/ ÷ sustained /z/. Above ~1.4 suggests the folds are not closing properly. Here: 0.72, comfortably healthy |
| **SOVT** | Semi-occluded vocal tract — any exercise partially blocking the airway (straw, lip trill, humming, /z/). Back-pressure props the folds into efficient vibration. Demonstrably effective here |
| **Pressed phonation** | Voicing with excessive fold compression and pressure. Harsh, spends air fast, fatigues quickly |
| **Masking** | Loud sound energy persisting briefly and drowning the quieter sound that follows. Why word-final consonants disappear and words appear to merge |
| **F0** | Fundamental frequency — the actual pitch, set by fold length and mass. Essentially fixed in an adult man |
| **Resonance** | How throat and mouth shape the sound after the folds produce it. Where *perceived* depth lives, and unlike F0 it is trainable |
| **Larynx height** | Vertical position of the voice box. Higher shortens the tract and raises resonance (thinner); lower lengthens it (deeper). Rises under load here |
| **Bone conduction** | Sound reaching your ears through your skull. Adds bass no listener hears |
| **Soft onset** | Beginning a word with airflow slightly ahead of the tone rather than a glottal click. A primary acoustic ingredient of politeness |
| **Prosody** | Pitch, pace, pause and rhythm layered over words. Carries emotion and attitude — a trainable skill, not a fixed trait |
