Where something is my recommendation and not a research finding, it is marked **(recommendation)**.

## 1. Articulation principles (14)

Each one: rule, why, before -> after, analogy.

**1. Say the answer first.**
Why: Minto's Pyramid Principle puts the main conclusion at the top, with supporting points below it [S1]. The US Army writing standard asks for "bottom line up front" (BLUF): main point at the start [S2].
Before: "So the vendor called, and then we checked the logs, and there was some issue with the API, so..." -> After: "The release will be two days late. The vendor's API broke our login."
Analogy: a newspaper headline. You read it first, details later.

**2. One point per sentence.**
Why: plain-language guidelines list "write short sentences" and keep subject, verb and object close together [S10].
Before: "The meeting, which was moved because the client, who had travel issues, couldn't join, is now Friday." -> After: "The client couldn't travel. We moved the meeting to Friday."
Analogy: one passenger per seat. Squeeze two in and nobody is comfortable.

**3. Use a short word when it does the job.**
Why: Orwell's rule 2: "Never use a long word where a short one will do" [S8]. Plain-language guidelines: "Use short, simple words" [S10].
Before: "We will endeavour to facilitate the commencement of the project." -> After: "We'll start the project."
Analogy: a key that fits the lock. A bigger key does not open it better.

**4. Cut words that add nothing.**
Why: Orwell's rule 3: if you can cut a word, cut it [S8]. The guidelines say "Omit unnecessary words" [S10].
Before: "At this point in time, I would just like to basically say that..." -> After: "Right now, ..."
Analogy: packing for a trip. Anything you won't use is just weight.

**5. Use active voice, so people can see who did what.**
Why: Orwell rule 4 [S8]. The Army standard pairs BLUF with active voice [S2]. The plain-language guidelines list "Use active voice" [S10].
Before: "The report was not sent." -> After: "I didn't send the report." / "Ravi didn't send the report."
Analogy: a CCTV clip that shows the face, not just the hand.

**6. Use a verb, not a noun that hides one.**
Why: the plain-language guidelines list "Avoid hidden verbs" (for example "make a decision" in place of "decide") [S10].
Before: "We did an analysis of the data and made a decision on the pricing." -> After: "We analysed the data and decided the price."
Analogy: a verb is the engine. A noun like "analysis" is a picture of an engine.

**7. Name the concrete thing.**
Why: Paivio's dual coding research found concrete, easy-to-picture words are remembered better than abstract ones [S24]. Listeners hold on to a picture longer than to a label.
Before: "There were some issues with the process." -> After: "Three orders shipped to the wrong city."
Analogy: "a fruit" vs "a ripe mango". Only one makes your mouth water.

**8. Start from what the listener needs.**
Why: Jay Sullivan's *Simply Said* starts with the listener: ask what they will hear and what they should remember [S7].
Before: "I worked on the dashboard code, refactored the charts, fixed the API..." -> After: "Sales can now see today's numbers without waiting for the morning email."
Analogy: a waiter tells you about the dish, not about the kitchen's day.

**9. Say why it matters.**
Why: Axios "Smart Brevity" puts a "Why it matters" line right after the opening line, adding meaning without repeating it [S6]. Abrahams' second question is "So what?" [S4].
Before: "We moved the server to Mumbai." -> After: "We moved the server to Mumbai. Pages now load twice as fast for Indian users."
Analogy: a price tag with no product name. The number means nothing until you know what it is for.

**10. Group your points in threes or fewer.**
Why: Minto groups the supporting points under each idea into small, clean groups, often called the "rule of three" in summaries of her method [S1].
Before: a list of seven reasons. -> After: "Three reasons: cost, speed and risk."
Analogy: a phone number is split into chunks so you can hold it in your head.

**11. End with the next step.**
Why: Abrahams' third question is "Now what?", the action or next step [S4].
Before: "...so that's the situation." -> After: "...so I need your OK on the budget by Thursday."
Analogy: a road sign that shows the distance and also the turn to take.

**12. Pause instead of filling silence.**
Why: Toastmasters has a meeting role, the "Ah-Counter", that counts crutch sounds ("ah", "um", "er") and words ("and", "well", "but", "so", "you know") [S11]. Research shows "uh" and "um" are real signals that a delay is coming [S12]. They are normal, but too many make you sound unsure. A short silent pause does the same job.
Before: "Um, so, basically, like, the thing is..." -> After: "[pause] The issue is the budget."
Analogy: a full stop on paper. In speech, the pause is the full stop.

**13. Drop the hedges you don't mean.**
Why: Smart Brevity's central idea is that being brief signals confidence [S6]. Orwell's rule 3 covers extra words like these too [S8].
Before: "I just kind of think maybe we should probably look at it." -> After: "I think we should review it."
Analogy: wearing three jackets on a warm day. One is enough.

**14. Use the same word for the same thing.**
Why: plain-language guidelines: "Use the same term consistently for a specific thought or object" [S10].
Before: "the client... the customer... the account... the party" (all the same person) -> After: "the client" every time.
Analogy: calling one friend by four names. People start to think there are four friends.

## 2. Frameworks for speaking on the spot

**PREP: Point, Reason, Example, Point.** Toastmasters material recommends it for impromptu "Table Topics" answers [S5]. Practice answers of 30 to 60 seconds [S5].
Q: "How was your day?"
- Point: "Productive. I finished the pricing page."
- Reason: "It was blocking the launch."
- Example: "I fixed the last bug at 4, and design signed off by 5."
- Point: "So we're on track for Monday."

**What / So what / Now what** (Matt Abrahams, *Think Faster, Talk Smarter*, Stanford) [S4]. What = the core idea; So what = why it matters to the listener; Now what = the next step.
Q: "What do you do?"
- What: "I build apps that help people learn."
- So what: "Right now I'm working on one that helps office workers speak more clearly in English."
- Now what: "If you know anyone who struggles in meetings, I'd love their feedback."

**SCQA: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer** (Minto) [S1]. This is how you open; the Answer becomes your main point.
Q: "Why did the project slip?"
- Situation: "We planned to launch on the 10th."
- Complication: "Two weeks ago, the payment provider changed its rules."
- Question (often unspoken): "So what did that mean for us?"
- Answer: "We had to rebuild checkout. That took eight days. New launch date: the 18th."

**BLUF: Bottom line up front** [S2].
Q: "Why did the project slip?"
"Bottom line: we're eight days late because the payment provider changed its rules. Checkout is rebuilt and tested. We launch on the 18th."
BLUF is the quick version of SCQA. Use it with bosses and busy people. Use SCQA when the listener needs context.

**Rule of three** [S1].
Q: "How was your day?"
"Three things: shipped the pricing page, had a good call with a customer, and cleared my inbox."

**One point per sentence** [S10].
Q: "What did you do today?"
Before: "I was doing the dashboard and also the meeting happened and some bugs I fixed and then the client call."
After: "I fixed two bugs on the dashboard. I had a call with the client. They liked the new charts."

**Which one to use (recommendation):** BLUF for status updates. PREP for opinions ("What do you think of X?"). What/So what/Now what for introductions and proposals. SCQA for explaining a problem.

## 3. Word-choice skills (20 before -> after pairs)

**A. Precise verbs over vague ones.** Plain-language guidance favours strong, simple verbs [S10].
1. "I did the meeting." -> "I ran the meeting." / "I led the meeting."
2. "I did some changes." -> "I made some changes." / "I changed the layout."
3. "We got a good response." -> "Users liked it: 40 signed up."
4. "I looked into the issue." -> "I found the cause: a wrong date format."
5. "I handled the client." -> "I calmed the client down and agreed a new date."

**B. Concrete over abstract** [S24].
6. "There was a lot of work." -> "I closed 14 tickets."
7. "The feedback was mixed." -> "Two of the five testers got lost on the sign-up page."
8. "There are some challenges." -> "We're short one designer."

**C. Remove fillers and hedges** [S8, S11].
9. "Basically, what I'm trying to say is the launch is late." -> "The launch is late."
10. "I just wanted to quickly check if maybe..." -> "Can you..."
11. "Actually, honestly speaking, I feel..." -> "I think..."

**D. Register: casual vs office.** The SJSU guide notes Indian English writing often keeps an older, wordy, flattering style; plain English prefers shorter sentences [S13].
12. "Please find attached herewith the document for your kind perusal." -> "I've attached the document."
13. "I have duly completed the work." -> "I've finished the work." [S13]
14. (casual) "What's up?" / (office) "How's the project going?" Both fine; don't mix ("Kindly tell me what's up").

**E. Collocations: words that go together.** Lewis's Lexical Approach says language is learned in chunks, not single words [S19]. Teaching learners to notice word groups made them sound more fluent to judges [S20].
15. "do a mistake" -> "make a mistake" [S13 lists "mistakes I have done"]
16. "take a decision" (British, fine) / "make a decision" (global, safest)
17. "heavy traffic", not "big traffic"
18. "raise a concern", not "put a concern"
19. "meet a deadline", not "achieve a deadline"
20. "strong coffee", not "powerful coffee"

## 4. How people actually learn vocabulary: what the research says

**Finding 1: You need many meetings with a word, and using a word takes more than recognising it.**
Webb (2007) showed learners a word 1, 3, 7 or 10 times. Recognition scores were always higher than production scores. Even 10 meetings gave big gains in recognising the word, but not enough to use it well [S14]. A 2019 meta-analysis (26 studies, 1,918 learners) found more repetitions mean more learning. It found no single magic number: the number needed depends on the learner and what "knowing" means [S15].
-> **App rule:** plan for 10 or more meetings with each word or phrase. Spread them across reading it, hearing it, recalling it and saying it.

**Finding 2: Recalling beats re-reading.**
Roediger & Karpicke (2006): students who were tested on material remembered much more one week later than students who re-read it. Re-reading only won on a test 5 minutes later, and it made students feel more confident than they should have [S16]. With 40 Swahili-English word pairs, extra testing helped a lot one week later; extra studying of already-learned words did nothing [S17]. A major review rated practice testing and spaced practice the two most useful study methods [S18].
-> **App rule:** every card ends with recall. Hide the answer, have him say or type it first, then show it. Never just "read and tap next".

**Finding 3: Space the reviews out, and make gaps longer as you go.**
Cepeda et al. (2006) pooled hundreds of studies: spreading practice out beats cramming [S21]. Cepeda et al. (2008, over 1,350 people) found the best gap grows with how long you want to remember. To remember for a week, the best gap was about 20-40% of that week. To remember for a year, it was about 5-10% of the year [S22]. With flashcards, one big spaced pile beat several small crammed piles for 90% of learners. Yet 72% believed cramming had worked better [S23].
-> **App rule:** a review ladder of roughly 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days, then about every 2-3 months (recommendation, built on [S22]). If he misses a recall, the word drops back down the ladder. Tell him that spacing feels harder but works better, so he doesn't drop it.

**Finding 4: Practise in the direction you want to get better at.**
Webb's word-pair studies: practice going from the meaning to the English word (productive) built the ability to produce the word. Practice going from the English word to the meaning (receptive) built recognition [S25]. Nation's framework says knowing a word has three parts (form, meaning and use), and each part can be known for recognising or for producing [S26].
-> **App rule:** since the goal is speaking, most practice should run from meaning to word: "Say this in English: you want to move the meeting earlier." -> "Can we bring the meeting forward?" Keep one recognition step at first exposure only.

**Finding 5: Making the word yourself helps it stick.**
Slamecka & Graf (1978): words people produced themselves (for example, from a clue and the first letter) were remembered better than the same words just read [S27]. The Involvement Load Hypothesis: tasks with more need, search and evaluation teach more. Writing your own sentence with the word scored highest [S28].
-> **App rule:** each new item gets a "your turn" step. He makes his own sentence about his real day or work, out loud or typed, and the AI checks it.

**Finding 6: Learn chunks, not loose words.**
Lewis: "language is grammaticalised lexis, not lexicalised grammar", meaning language is mostly ready-made word groups [S19]. In a small study of 32 students, the group taught to notice word groups was rated more fluent by blind judges [S20]. The British Council links fluency to fast access to chunks [S19].
-> **App rule:** the unit of learning is the phrase ("meet a deadline", "bring forward", "on track"), not the single word.

**Finding 7: A picture plus a word beats a word alone.**
Dual coding: things stored as both words and images are recalled better. Concrete words beat abstract ones [S24].
-> **App rule:** each card's analogy should paint a picture. For abstract words, attach a concrete scene ("on track" -> a train on time).

**Finding 8: Balance the four strands.**
Nation's "four strands": a good course gives roughly equal time to input (listening, reading), output (speaking, writing), deliberate study, and fluency practice [S29]. Flashcards cover only deliberate study.
-> **App rule:** pair cards with a daily speaking prompt ("Tell me about your day in 3 sentences using BLUF") so new words get used, not only reviewed.

**How many new items a day? (recommendation)** The research gives no single number [S15]. Producing a word costs more than recognising it [S14], and each new item brings about 6 reviews over the following months. For one busy adult aiming to *speak* the words: **3-5 new items a day, capped at around 25 new a week, and no new cards on days with more than 30 reviews due.** At this rate the daily review load settles around 20-30 cards (10-15 minutes). Growing slowly beats quitting.

## 5. Common Indian-English mistakes (54)

**Framing (important):** Linguists treat Indian English as a valid variety with stable rules of its own [S13]. The goal is not "Indian = wrong". It is "be understood instantly by any listener, in any country". Cards should say "In global English, people say..." and not "This is wrong".

Source key: [S13] SJSU guide (Khan; built on Sailaja 2009, Mesthrie & Bhatt 2008), [S30] Tharoor, [S31] Tharoor, [S32] eWAVE, [S33] Oxford Learner's, [S34] Merriam-Webster, [S35] Grammarly, [S36] Grammarphobia, [S37] Nihalani et al. via LINGUIST List, [S38]/[S39] on "revert", [S40] Merriam-Webster "discuss", [S41] Talkdrill (secondary).

**Verbs and tense**
1. "I am having a car." -> "I have a car." Have/know/believe describe states; they don't usually take -ing. [S13, S32]
2. "I am knowing him for years." -> "I've known him for years." Same rule, plus "for years" needs have + -ed. [S32, S13]
3. "I was not knowing this." -> "I didn't know this." [S13]
4. "I am believing you." -> "I believe you." [S13]
5. "I have bought the book yesterday." -> "I bought the book yesterday." With a finished past time (yesterday), use simple past. [S13]
6. "I am working here since five years." -> "I've been working here for five years." "For" + length of time; "since" + starting point. [S41]
7. "She know JavaScript." -> "She knows JavaScript." He/she/it takes -s. [S13]
8. "The next step would be to test it." (meaning a plan) -> "The next step will be to test it." "Would" sounds unsure or hypothetical. [S13]
9. "We enjoyed very much." -> "We really enjoyed it." "Enjoy" needs an object. [S13]
10. "I didn't expect." -> "I didn't expect that." [S13]

**Questions**
11. "Which way you are going?" -> "Which way are you going?" Flip subject and helper verb in questions. [S13]
12. "When you will be there?" -> "When will you be there?" [S13]
13. "They're coming tomorrow, isn't it?" -> "...aren't they?" The tag matches the verb and person. [S13]
14. "You had seen it, isn't it?" -> "You'd seen it, hadn't you?" [S13]

**Countable/uncountable and plurals**
15. "luggages, baggages" -> "luggage, bags". [S13]
16. "equipments, furnitures" -> "equipment, furniture". [S13]
17. "informations" -> "information". [S13, S37]
18. "feedbacks" -> "feedback". [S13]
19. "trainings / researches" -> "training sessions / research". [S13]
20. "I have a good news." -> "I have some good news." "News" takes no "a". [S13]
21. "Let's go through the alphabets." -> "...the letters of the alphabet." [S13]

**Articles, prepositions, small words**
22. "I am master's student of CS." -> "I'm a master's student in CS." [S13]
23. "She gave me one book." -> "She gave me a book." [S13]
24. "Let's discuss about the plan." -> "Let's discuss the plan." "Discuss" already means "talk about". [S13, S40]
25. "Pay attention on this." -> "Pay attention to this." [S13]
26. "the below software" -> "the software below". [S13]
27. "cope up with stress" -> "cope with stress". [S37]

**Emphasis and repetition**
28. "I'll send it today itself." -> "I'll send it today." / "...by end of day today." [S13]
29. "I'm here only." -> "I'm right here." [S13]
30. "small small things" -> "really small things". [S13]
31. "three-four people" -> "three or four people". [S13]
32. "This coffee is too good." -> "This coffee is really good." "Too" means "more than is OK". [S13]

**Words with a different meaning abroad**
33. "Please revert." / "revert back" -> "Please reply." / "I'll get back to you." Elsewhere "revert" means "go back to an earlier state". "Back" repeats what "revert" already means. [S13, S38, S39]
34. "Please do the needful." -> "Please [name the action]: approve the invoice by Friday." Rarely used outside India and vague. [S13, S30, S35]
35. "Can we prepone the meeting?" -> "Can we bring the meeting forward?" / "move it earlier". Dictionaries label "prepone" Indian English. [S13, S31, S34]
36. "I'm out of station." -> "I'm out of town." [S13, S30]
37. "I passed out of college in 2015." -> "I graduated in 2015." "Passed out" means "fainted". [S13]
38. "I have a doubt." -> "I have a question." [S13]
39. "His uncle expired." -> "His uncle passed away." "Expired" is for milk and passports. [S13]
40. "I'll call her to my party." -> "I'll invite her." [S13]
41. "Mistakes I have done" -> "Mistakes I've made." [S13]
42. "cent percent" -> "a hundred percent". [S13, S30]
43. "I'm writing/giving an exam." -> "I'm taking an exam." [S13]
44. "We're shifting house." -> "We're moving house." [S13]
45. "Let's eat at that hotel." -> "...that restaurant." [S13]
46. "Mugging for exams" -> "cramming for exams". "Mugging" abroad means robbery. [S13, S31]
47. "my cousin brother" -> "my cousin". [S33]
48. "my co-brother" -> "my wife's sister's husband" / "my brother-in-law". [S13, S30]
49. "I have to by-heart it." -> "I have to learn it by heart / memorise it." [S37]
50. "updation of records" -> "updating records" / "an update". [S36]

**Phone, social, idiom**
51. "Open/close the light." -> "Turn on/off the light." [S13]
52. "What's your good name?" -> "What's your name?" / "May I have your name?" [S13]
53. "Hello, Ramu this side." / "Myself Salman." -> "Hi, it's Ramu." / "I'm Salman." [S30]
54. "Mention not." / "cut the call" / "years back" / "he's eating my head" -> "You're welcome." / "hang up" / "years ago" / "he's annoying me". [S30, S13]

**Style (writing and formal speech):** "kindly", "duly", "esteemed", "aforesaid", "the same" (as a pronoun) and flattering openers sound old-fashioned or over-polite to global listeners. Use short, direct sentences [S13].

## 6. Recommended vocabulary categories for the app

Rule for keeping categories separate: **sort each item by what it does in a sentence**, not by topic. Each item belongs to exactly one category. Topics (meetings, travel, family) become tags.

| # | Category | What goes in | Example items | Not this |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Precise verbs** | Single verbs that replace "do/get/make/handle" | lead, fix, launch, flag, postpone, approve | Phrasal verbs (go to 2) |
| 2 | **Phrasal verbs** | Verb + particle with its own meaning | bring forward, follow up, sort out, run into, push back | Fixed idioms (go to 4) |
| 3 | **Collocations** | Pairs that sound natural together; the meaning is clear from the words | meet a deadline, raise a concern, heavy traffic, make a mistake | Anything with a hidden meaning |
| 4 | **Idioms** | Fixed phrases where the meaning is NOT the sum of the words | on the same page, ballpark figure, the ball is in your court | Rare or old idioms ("raining cats and dogs") |
| 5 | **Office phrases** | Whole ready-made sentences for work moments | "Just to recap...", "Can we park that?", "I'll get back to you by Friday." | Single words |
| 6 | **Connectors** | Words that link ideas and signal structure | so, because, however, on top of that, as a result, in short | Fillers |
| 7 | **Feelings & reactions** | Precise emotion words and reaction phrases | relieved, frustrated, overwhelmed, "That's great news!", "That must be tough." | Business jargon |
| 8 | **Natural thinking-time phrases** | Short phrases that buy a second without sounding unsure [S12] | "Good question.", "Let me think.", "The short answer is...", "Off the top of my head..." | um/like/basically (these are what we're replacing) |
| 9 | **Indian-English fixes** | Each mistake in section 5, as a pair: usual form -> global form | revert -> reply; prepone -> bring forward | New vocabulary (each fix links to its category above) |

Mix (recommendation): about 60% from categories 1-3 and 5 (daily usefulness), 20% from 6-8 (fluency and warmth), 10% idioms, 10% fixes.

**Tiering.** Beck, McKeown & Kucan sort words into three tiers. Tier 1 is basic everyday words, tier 3 is specialist terms, and tier 2 sits between: useful words that mature speakers use in many settings. Tier 2 is where teaching pays off most [S42]. Target tier-2 items: "postpone", "outcome", "prioritise", "clarify". Avoid tier 3 ("amortisation") unless he asks.

## 7. Rules for the AI when it generates cards

**Quality bar for every card**
1. **One idea per card.** One rule or one phrase, never two.
2. **Common, useful words only.** The item must be something a fluent office worker would say in a normal week. No showy or rare words ("perspicacious", "obfuscate"). Test: would a colleague raise an eyebrow? Then drop it.
3. **Tier 2 first** [S42]. Not baby words, not specialist jargon.
4. **Teach the chunk, not the lone word** [S19, S20]. Every word appears inside a natural phrase.
5. **Examples must sound like real speech.** Sentence one is about work (meeting, email, stand-up, client call). Sentence two is about daily life. Each under 15 words. Indian context is welcome (a chai break, Diwali leave, Bengaluru traffic), but the English must be global.
6. **Show before -> after** for articulation and fix cards: what he might say now, then the better version.
7. **The analogy must be concrete and visual** [S24]. One line. No analogy that needs its own explanation.
8. **The rule line is 12 words or fewer** and uses only basic words.
9. **Every card ends in recall** [S16, S17]. Write a prompt that goes from meaning to English ("How would you say: ...?") so he produces the phrase [S25].
10. **Every new phrase gets a "your turn" step:** make your own sentence about your day [S27, S28].
11. **Correct gently and label the variety.** For Indian-English items, say "In global English..." and never mock [S13].
12. **Register tag on every phrase:** casual / neutral / formal. Never mix registers inside one example.
13. **No invented usage.** Each phrase must exist in a major learner's dictionary (Oxford Learner's, Cambridge, Longman, Merriam-Webster) with the same meaning. If unsure, don't generate it. (Ties to the existing SPEAK content-quality gate.)
14. **No duplicates or near-duplicates** of anything already in his deck (check the meaning, not just the spelling).
15. **Spread new items across categories.** No more than 2 new items from the same category per day; follow the daily cap in section 4.
16. **Grammar checks before output:** the right "a/an/the", countable vs uncountable, tag questions, and no "-ing" on state verbs. The model must not commit the very errors in section 5.
17. **Articulation cards name the framework** (BLUF, PREP, What/So what/Now what, SCQA) and show it answering a real everyday question.
18. **Don't teach "never say um".** Teach a pause or a thinking-time phrase in its place. Fillers are normal speech signals [S12]; the goal is fewer of them, not zero.
19. **British vs American:** default to neutral international forms. When they differ ("take a decision" vs "make a decision"), teach the form understood everywhere and mention the other.
20. **Self-check before output:** rule <= 12 words; 2 examples; analogy present; recall prompt present; register tag present; no rare words; no section-5 errors.
