# Storytelling in everyday speech: research for SPEAK cards

Who it's for: one adult learner. Indian office worker, English is his second language, builds products and pitches ideas. Goal: tell everyday stories well. That means his day, anecdotes with friends, incidents at work, pitches, and introducing himself.

How sure each claim is:
- **[V]** I checked it against a primary source or more than one good secondary source.
- **[S]** Only secondary summaries say it. The idea is right, but the exact wording may be off.
- **[!]** Shaky or disputed. Don't put it on a card as fact.

---

## 1. Core principles (14)

Each one has a rule, a reason, an example and an analogy.

**P1. A story is about a change, not a list of events.**
- Why: Matthew Dicks (*Storyworthy*) says every story needs a "five-second moment" where the teller changes (transformation) or understands something new (realization). He says the rest of the story is there to set up that moment [S]. The Moth's stage rules also say a story should show some kind of change [V].
- Example: "I walked into that review sure I'd be praised. I walked out knowing I'd never once asked the client what they wanted."
- Analogy: A photo of a cricket ball sitting still is boring. A photo of the stumps flying is a story.

**P2. Start at the opposite of where you end.**
- Why: Dicks says to find your moment of change, work out its opposite, and open there. The gap between the two is the arc [S]. One critic notes this rule can be overused, so treat it as a rule of thumb [S].
- Example: If the story ends "…and now I always ask before I build," start with "I used to think asking users was a waste of time."
- Analogy: A before/after weight-loss photo works only because the "before" is shown.

**P3. No stakes, no story.**
- Why: The Moth's official tips ask what you stand to gain or lose. Without stakes, they say, it's an essay and belongs on the page, not the stage [V]. Dicks treats stakes as non-negotiable and humour as optional [S].
- Example: "If this demo crashed, the client would walk, and so would my promotion."
- Analogy: Watching a cricket match you don't care about vs. a final with India batting.

**P4. Connect events with "but" and "therefore", not "and then".**
- Why: South Park's creators described a "rule of replacing": swap "and" for "but" or "therefore" [S]. Randy Olson built his And-But-Therefore (ABT) template on it [V]. Dicks gives the same advice: replace "and then" with "but" and "therefore" [S].
- Example: Weak: "I reached office, and then the meeting started, and then the client called." Strong: "I reached office early, *but* the client called furious, *therefore* I rewrote the whole deck before 10."
- Analogy: "And then" is a flat road. "But" is a speed-breaker. "Therefore" is the turn you take because of it.

**P5. Open in the action, not the weather.**
- Why: The Moth asks for a first line that sets up the stakes and grabs attention [V]. Dicks advises against opening with background [S].
- Example: Weak: "So yesterday was a normal Tuesday, I woke up at seven…" Strong: "At 9:58 my laptop died. The CEO's call was at 10."
- Analogy: A good film doesn't show the hero brushing his teeth unless the toothbrush matters.

**P6. Concrete details beat general words.**
- Why: *Made to Stick* (Chip & Dan Heath) lists Concrete as one of six traits of ideas that stick (SUCCESs: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories) [V]. Kindra Hall lists specific details as one of four parts of a sticky story [S]. Vivid detail also makes a story believable from the inside [S, Heath].
- Example: Weak: "The traffic was bad." Strong: "Forty minutes on Silk Board flyover, and the auto driver switched off the engine to save petrol."
- Analogy: "Food was nice" vs. "the dal had that smoky tadka your mother makes."

**P7. Know your last line before you start.**
- Why: The Moth says your last line should be clear in your head before you begin, and that meandering endings "kill a story" [V]. Dicks says to start building a story from its end [S]. Moth coaches suggest learning the first and last lines word for word and keeping the middle as loose bullet points [S].
- Example: Plan the last line ("That's the day I stopped saying 'quick question' to my manager") and steer everything towards it.
- Analogy: A cab driver who knows the destination takes a straight route. One who doesn't, circles.

**P8. Tell it like you'd tell a friend at dinner.**
- Why: Dicks's "Dinner Test": your meeting or stage version should be close to the version you'd tell a friend over dinner. No theatrical gestures, no overwrought language [S].
- Example: Not "I was confronted with an unprecedented challenge" but "I panicked. Properly panicked."
- Analogy: Wear your good shirt, not a costume.

**P9. Raise stakes by sharing the plan before it breaks.**
- Why: Dicks lists five ways to build stakes [S]:
  - Elephant: say the big problem early.
  - Backpack: share your plan or hope before it goes wrong.
  - Breadcrumbs: drop hints of what's coming.
  - Hourglass: slow down at the tense part.
  - Crystal ball: make a prediction the listener wonders about.
- Example (backpack): "My plan was simple: present at 3, leave by 4, reach my cousin's engagement by 6." Now the listener feels every delay.
- Analogy: You only fear for the egg once you know someone plans to carry it up the stairs.

**P10. Slow down at the moment that matters.**
- Why: Dicks's "hourglass": stretch time at peak tension [S]. Kindra Hall also stresses a significant moment told in specific detail [S].
- Example: "He opened my file. Scrolled. Stopped. Took off his glasses. And said… 'Who wrote this?'"
- Analogy: Slow-motion replay of the last ball.

**P11. One story, one point.**
- Why: *Made to Stick*'s first trait is Simple: find the core and drop everything else [V].
- Example: A failure story about a missed deadline teaches *one* lesson ("I said yes too fast"), not five.
- Analogy: A tiffin with one great sabzi beats a thali of ten average ones.

**P12. Reflect, then return to action.**
- Why: The Moth's book says its stories alternate between action and reflection [S].
- Example: "I sent the email. (Looking back, I should have slept on it.) Two minutes later my phone rang."
- Analogy: Cricket commentary. Ball, then a line of opinion, then the next ball.

**P13. Stories persuade better than bare facts, but only a bit, and only if people get absorbed.**
- Why: Green & Brock (2000) found that the more "transported" (absorbed) readers were, the more their beliefs moved towards the story's [V]. A meta-analysis by Braddock & Dillard (2016) found small but real effects of narratives on beliefs, attitudes, intentions and behaviour (r ≈ .17–.23) [V].
- Example: In a pitch, "Ramesh, a chemist in Indore, writes every order in a notebook" plus one number beats ten numbers.
- Analogy: A trailer sells the film better than its budget sheet. You still need the budget sheet.

**P14. Kill the curse of knowledge: explain from the listener's chair.**
- Why: *Made to Stick* calls the "curse of knowledge" the main villain. Once you know something, you can't imagine not knowing it [V, book's central idea]. Michael Seibel (YC) test: write two sentences about what you do. If someone can't explain it back, "you are wrong, not them" [S].
- Example: Not "we're a B2B SaaS for SKU-level demand forecasting" but "we tell kirana shops what to stock next week."
- Analogy: Giving directions to a tourist, not to your neighbour.

**On "storytelling releases oxytocin" [!].** Paul Zak's lab reported that character-driven videos raised blood oxytocin and that this predicted donations (summarised in HBR, 2014) [V that he claimed it]. But:
- His earlier headline finding (oxytocin increases trust, 2005) has replicated poorly. See Nave, Camerer & McCullough (2015) and the failed replications by Lane et al. (2015) [V].
- McCullough, Churchland & Mendez (2013) showed that the common blood-oxytocin tests are unreliable and measure other molecules too [V].
- So no card should say "stories release oxytocin" as fact. If the topic comes up at all, say "some researchers claim, but it's disputed".
- Use transportation theory and the meta-analysis (P13) as the evidence base instead.

## 2. Story structures, with bad vs good worked examples

### 2.1 ABT: And, But, Therefore (Randy Olson) [V]
Shape: "[Setup] AND [more setup], BUT [problem], THEREFORE [what we did]." Best for one-breath explanations and the first line of a pitch.
- **Bad (54 words):** "So we had the quarterly review and we had all the slides and there were many teams presenting and our team was fourth and we showed the numbers and the numbers were okay-ish and then the VP asked some questions and then we answered and then it got over around six."
- **Good (55 words):** "We'd spent two weeks on the quarterly review deck, and we were sure the numbers looked solid. But ten minutes in, the VP asked one question: 'Why did repeat users drop?' Nobody had checked. Therefore, instead of a pat on the back, we left with a 48-hour deadline to find out."

### 2.2 Story Spine (Kenn Adams; made famous by Emma Coats's "Pixar rules", #4) [V]
Shape: "Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally…"

Note: Coats tweeted these in 2011 as a Pixar storyboard artist. They're widely called "Pixar's rules", but they were her personal notes, not an official Pixar document [S]. The spine itself comes from improv teacher Kenn Adams [S].
- **Bad (45 words):** "I take the 8:10 metro. Today there was a delay. I was late. My manager saw. I explained. Then I worked on the report. Then lunch. Then I finished the report and went home. It was a long day."
- **Good (75 words):** "Every day I take the 8:10 metro and reach before my manager. One day the metro stops dead between stations for twenty minutes. Because of that, I walk in at 9:40, straight into her. Because of that, I mumble an excuse she clearly doesn't believe. So I stay till eight to finish the report early. Until finally, she replies at 8:15: 'This is the best one yet. Late more often.'"

### 2.3 The five-second moment (Dicks) [S]
Shape: Before (the opposite) → stakes → the moment of change → after.
- **Bad (48 words):** "I learned a lot about leadership today. Leadership is about listening. Our junior designer suggested something in the meeting and it was good. So I think leaders should always listen to junior people, because they have good ideas, and that's an important lesson for everyone."
- **Good (72 words):** "For three years I believed the person with the most experience in the room should talk first. Today, in the design review, our intern Priya raised her hand. I almost said 'later'. Then she said, 'Users don't scroll that far. I watched my mom try.' Silence. She was right, and I'd missed it for six months. I'm the one who almost said 'later'."

### 2.4 STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result (career centres) [V]
Use it for interviews and "tell me about a time" questions. Say "I" not "we". Spend about 20% on Situation, about 10% on Task, and most of the time on Action and Result (University of Washington guidance).
- **Bad (45 words):** "In my last job we had a lot of problems with a vendor and the team worked really hard and we managed it and in the end it was fine and the client was happy and we learned a lot about vendor management."
- **Good (78 words):** "Situation: our payment vendor's API was failing on 1 in 20 transactions, a week before Diwali sales. Task: I owned checkout, so stopping lost orders was my job. Action: I added a silent retry and a backup vendor switch, and I called their CTO directly instead of raising tickets. Result: failures dropped to 1 in 500, and we didn't lose a single Diwali order. I learned to escalate by phone, not by ticket."

### 2.5 Problem, Solution, Why now (from Sequoia's "Writing a Business Plan" outline) [S]
The outline runs: company purpose in one sentence → problem → solution → why now → market → competition → product → business model → team → financials.
- **Bad (50 words):** "So our idea is a platform which leverages AI to provide end-to-end solutions for the retail ecosystem, enabling stakeholders to optimise operations and drive growth through data-driven insights, and it has many features like dashboards and alerts and integrations."
- **Good (71 words):** "Kirana owners guess what to stock. My uncle in Jaipur throws away about ₹4,000 of expired milk and bread every month. We built a WhatsApp bot. He sends a photo of his shelf, and it tells him what to reorder tomorrow. Why now? UPI means every sale is already digital, and phone cameras are good enough to read a shelf. Twelve shops tried it. Waste fell by a third."

### 2.6 Situation, Complication, Resolution (Minto's SCQA, from *The Pyramid Principle*) [V]
Shape: Situation (what we all know) → Complication (what changed) → Question (what it raises) → Answer, which you lead with in business. Best for meetings and status updates.
- **Bad (52 words):** "So I looked at the data and there were a few things, the traffic, the conversion, mobile is different from desktop, we also have the new release, and some customers complained, so after looking at everything I think maybe we should consider rolling back the release, possibly."
- **Good (60 words):** "Short version: we should roll back yesterday's release. Situation: sign-ups were steady at around 400 a day. Complication: since the release, mobile sign-ups fell to 150, and desktop didn't move. So the question is whether it's the release or something else. The answer: only the mobile form changed. Rolling back costs an hour. Waiting costs about 250 users a day."

### 2.7 Hook, Body, Punchline (anecdote shape; Moth and comedy practice)
Shape: hook line → quick setup → escalation → punchline or callback, then stop.
- **Bad (46 words):** "Funny thing happened. I went to a meeting, it was online, there was a client, and I thought I was on mute but I wasn't, and I said something about lunch, and everybody heard it, so it was embarrassing but funny."
- **Good (64 words):** "I said 'paneer' to a Fortune 500 client today. Forty minutes into their budget call, I'm sure I'm on mute, and I tell my wife, 'Make the paneer, this guy will never stop talking.' Silence. Then their CFO unmutes: 'Which paneer? Butter or kadai?' We closed the deal. He wants the recipe."

### 2.8 Normal, Explosion, New normal (Kindra Hall, *Stories That Stick*) [S]
Shape: how things usually were → the event that broke it → how things are now. Best for "why I started this" founder stories and customer stories.
- **Example (60 words):** "Normal: I spent every Sunday night building Monday's sales report by hand, three hours of copy-paste. Explosion: one Sunday my daughter asked, 'Papa, why do you work on holidays?' I had no good answer. New normal: I wrote a script that builds it in four minutes. Now six teams use it, and Sunday is cricket again."

## 3. Micro-skills: before and after lines

| Skill | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook / opening line** (Moth: first line sets stakes) | "So, um, yesterday something happened at work." | "Yesterday I almost got fired for being *too* helpful." |
| **State stakes early** (Dicks's elephant) | "I had a presentation." | "One presentation decided if our team survived the reorg." |
| **Backpack (plan before it breaks)** | "Then the cab didn't come." | "Plan: cab at 7, airport by 8, flight at 9:30. At 7:20, the app said 'driver cancelled'." |
| **Specific detail** (Heath: Concrete) | "The client was angry." | "The client typed in all caps: 'WHO APPROVED THIS?'" |
| **Dialogue instead of report** | "He said that he wasn't happy with it." | "He leaned back and said, 'Is this the final version?'" |
| **Present tense for the key scene** (Dicks) | "I walked in and the room went quiet." | "I walk in. The room goes quiet." |
| **But / therefore** (ABT) | "…and then the server went down, and then we called IT." | "…*but* the server went down, *so* I called IT from my personal phone." |
| **Pacing: the hourglass pause** | "He read it and said it was wrong." | "He reads it. [pause] Reads it again. [pause] 'Who wrote this?'" |
| **The turn (five-second moment)** | "So I realised listening matters." | "And right there I realise: I haven't asked her a single question in three years." |
| **Ending / callback** | "So yeah, that's it, it was a crazy day." | (Opened with "I hate Mondays") "…and that's the first Monday I didn't hate." |
| **Cut the boring bits** | "I woke up, had chai, took the bus, reached office, opened laptop, and then…" | "At 10:02, my laptop shows a blue screen." |
| **One point only** (Heath: Simple) | "…and I learned about patience, teamwork, planning, and also to back up files." | "…and I learned one thing: back up before the demo, not after." |
| **Show, don't label emotion** | "I was very nervous." | "My hand is shaking so hard the clicker rattles." |
| **Reflection beat** (Moth: action and reflection) | (no reflection) | "I hit send. Even now I don't know why I didn't wait till morning." |
| **Plain words over jargon** (Seibel: clarity over flair) | "We leveraged synergies to optimise outcomes." | "We shared one delivery van and saved ₹30,000 a month." |
| **Contrast / surprise** (Heath: Unexpected) | "The new intern was good." | "The intern, on day two, found a bug our senior team missed for a year." |

Notes for the learner on the English (the second-language angle):
- Use the present tense consistently inside a scene. Don't mix "I walk in and he said".
- Use short sentences at the tense moment.
- "So", "but" and "and then" are fine to say out loud. Just make sure "but" and "so" carry the turns.

## 4. Story types he needs, with templates

**4.1 "How was your day?" (30–45 seconds)**
- Template: *One moment, not the whole day.* "[Hook: the single most surprising or annoying moment]. [Quick setup: where, who]. [But: what went wrong or surprised you]. [So: what you did]. [One-line feeling or punchline]."
- Example: "Best part of today? The auto driver gave *me* change advice. I hand him ₹500 for a ₹90 ride, and he says, 'Sir, keep small notes, Bengaluru runs on UPI and patience.' Then he wouldn't take UPI. So I walked to a chai stall to break the note, and the chai-wala gave me the same lecture."
- Rule: ditch the timeline. Pick one moment (Homework for Life trains this).

**4.2 Funny anecdote (45–90 seconds)**
- Template: hook → setup with a backpack (what you expected) → escalation in 2–3 beats, each worse → punchline → STOP (don't explain the joke).
- Moth: comic material must be narrative, not a stand-up routine [V].

**4.3 Failure / lesson story (60–90 seconds)**
- Template: "I used to believe [opposite of lesson]. [Situation + stakes]. [What I did, confidently]. But [how it failed: specific, with one line of dialogue]. [Five-second moment]. Now I [concrete changed behaviour]."
- Lead with your own fault, not other people's. Annette Simmons calls this a "Teaching story" [S].

**4.4 Pitch: 30 seconds**
- Template (ABT + Seibel): "[Who] [does what], and [it matters because…]. But [specific pain, with a number or a person]. So we built [one-line what it does, in plain words]. [One proof point]."
- Example: "Small clinics in tier-2 cities book appointments on paper, and patients still wait two hours. But half of those patients already message the clinic on WhatsApp. So we built a WhatsApp queue: you get a message when it's your turn. Eight clinics, average wait down from 2 hours to 25 minutes."

**4.5 Pitch: 2 minutes**
- Template:
  1. Hook / one-sentence purpose
  2. Problem told as one real person's story (Normal → Explosion)
  3. Solution, and what it looks like in use
  4. Why now
  5. Proof: users, numbers
  6. What's next / the ask
- Tie it back to the person from step 2 at the end (a callback).
- Use Duarte's "what is vs. what could be" contrast: alternate the painful present with the better future [V for the contrast idea; "new bliss" wording unverified].

**4.6 Explaining an incident at work (to a manager or in a meeting)**
- Template (SCQA, answer first): "Bottom line: [status / what I need]. What happened: [Situation]. What went wrong: [Complication, with a timestamp and specific facts]. What I did: [actions]. Now: [current state + next step + what I need from you]."
- Tone: facts before feelings, "I" for your own actions, no blame-chain.

**4.7 Introducing yourself (30 seconds)**
- Template (Simmons's "Who I am" story [S] + Dicks's opposite-start): "[Name, role in plain words]. [One short story or moment that shows why you do this]. [What you're working on now / what you're looking for]."
- Example: "I'm Arjun. I build tools for small shops. I got into it when I watched my father close his medical store's accounts by hand every night till midnight. Now I'm building something so no shop-owner has to do that."

## 5. Layered curriculum (Level 1 → 5)

**Level 1: What makes it a story (basics)**
1. A story = someone wants something + something gets in the way.
2. Story vs. report: "and then" vs. "but / so".
3. One moment, not the whole day.
4. Every story has a change (the five-second moment).
5. Start in the action, not with "So yesterday…".
6. Know your last line first.
7. Past vs. present tense: pick one and stay in it.

**Level 2: Making it vivid**
1. Swap a general word for a specific one ("vehicle" → "Activa").
2. Name the person, the place, the time ("9:58 a.m.").
3. One line of real dialogue.
4. Show the feeling through the body (shaking hands, not "nervous").
5. The five senses: pick one.
6. Cut three boring sentences from the start.
7. Short sentences at the tense moment.

**Level 3: Tension and stakes**
1. Stakes: what could be lost? Say it early (elephant).
2. Backpack: share the plan, then break it.
3. Breadcrumbs: hint at what's coming.
4. Hourglass: slow down at the peak.
5. Crystal ball: make a prediction.
6. Surprise: set up an expectation, then flip it.
7. The pause: where to stop talking for a second.

**Level 4: Shapes and endings**
1. ABT in one breath.
2. Story spine (once / every day / one day / because of that / until finally).
3. Opposite start → opposite end.
4. Callback endings.
5. Action ↔ reflection rhythm.
6. Shapes of stories:
   - Vonnegut's "man in a hole": things get bad, then better.
   - Reagan et al. (2016) found six common emotional arcs in 1,327 novels [V].
   - Use the shapes as simple pictures, not as rules.
7. One story, one point. Cut the second lesson.
8. The Dinner Test: does it sound like you?

**Level 5: Stories that work (business and persuasion)**
1. STAR for interviews.
2. SCQA / answer-first for meetings.
3. The incident report story.
4. 30-second pitch (ABT + Seibel's two sentences).
5. 2-minute pitch (problem → solution → why now → proof → ask).
6. Founder story (Normal → Explosion → New normal).
7. Customer story: tell the problem through one real person.
8. "I know what you're thinking": answer the doubt before it's raised (Simmons).
9. Data + story: one number inside one human moment.
10. Curse of knowledge: the jargon-cutting drill.

## 6. Daily practice habit: Homework for Life, adapted

**Source:** Dicks's Homework for Life. Every night, write down the most storyworthy moment of your day, even if it seems trivial [S]. Sources differ on how long this takes (about 2 vs. about 5 minutes), so keep it tiny.

**App design (about 3–5 minutes a day):**
1. **Night prompt (60 s):** "What's one moment from today you'd tell a friend?" One line, typed or spoken. No full story, just the moment. Keep a streak.
2. **Morning card (60 s):** One rule card (one rule + 1–2 examples + an analogy) from the current level.
3. **Apply it (60–90 s):** Re-tell last night's moment *using today's rule only* (e.g. "add one line of dialogue"). Speak it aloud.
4. **Feedback (30 s):** Check against that one rule only. Show a one-line "before → after" rewrite of his own sentence.
5. **Weekly:** Pick the best of seven moments → build it into a full 60–90 s story with the week's skills. Save it to a "story bank". Dicks recommends building a stash of ready stories for business use [S].
6. **Monthly:** Tell one bank story as a pitch, intro or meeting story (a type from section 4). The goal is to reuse stories across situations.

**Why it should work:** Small daily recall builds the habit of noticing moments, which Dicks says is the hard part. One rule at a time keeps the load low for a second-language speaker. Spaced re-telling of the same stories lets fluency build on known content.

## 7. Rules the AI must follow when generating storytelling cards

1. **One rule per card.** State the rule in one plain sentence (≤ 15 words). No stacking.
2. **Always a before/after pair** from an ordinary Indian office or daily-life setting (metro, auto, chai, standup, manager, client call, kirana, family WhatsApp). The "after" must differ from the "before" *only* in the skill being taught.
3. **Examples are short:** a single line, or 40–90 words for a mini-story. Simple vocabulary (aim CEFR B1–B2). No idioms unless the card is teaching them.
4. **Everyday analogy:** concrete and familiar (cricket, cooking, traffic, Bollywood, family). Never abstract.
5. **Attribute frameworks correctly.** Use only these pairings:
   - ABT = Randy Olson, from South Park's "but/therefore" rule
   - Story Spine = Kenn Adams, popularised by Emma Coats
   - Five-second moment / Homework for Life / Dinner Test / elephant-backpack-breadcrumbs-hourglass-crystal ball = Matthew Dicks
   - SUCCESs / curse of knowledge = Chip & Dan Heath
   - SCQA / Pyramid = Barbara Minto
   - Normal–Explosion–New normal = Kindra Hall
   - Six story types = Annette Simmons
   - What is / what could be = Nancy Duarte
   - Six arcs = Reagan et al. 2016 (after Vonnegut)
   
   Don't invent quotes. Paraphrase.
6. **No neuroscience hype.** Never say "stories release oxytocin / dopamine" as fact. If science is cited, use Green & Brock (transportation) or Braddock & Dillard (small but real persuasion effect).
7. **Grammar must be correct in every example**, especially tense consistency. This follows the SPEAK content quality gate: a generated card must pass a grammar and fact check before it is shown.
8. **True-to-life, not melodrama.** Stakes should be office-sized (a deadline, a demo, a manager's opinion). Don't invent tragedy.
9. **No jargon in examples** unless the card is about cutting jargon. Then show the jargon line as the "before".
10. **Respect the level order.** Don't use a Level 3 term (backpack, hourglass) on a Level 1 card.
11. **Every "after" ends cleanly.** No "so yeah, that's it" unless it's the bad example.
12. **Feedback on his speech targets only that card's rule**, quotes his actual words, and gives one rewritten line. It is not a full critique.
13. **Cultural fit:** Indian English is fine. Don't "correct" valid Indian English usage (e.g. "prepone") in a storytelling card. That belongs in grammar cards, if anywhere.
