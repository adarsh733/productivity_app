# Small Talk and Conversation: Research for SPEAK

Researched 2026-10-29. For one learner: an Indian office worker and product builder who speaks English as a second language. His problem: when someone asks "How was your day?" he says "Nice, good" and the conversation stops.

**How much to trust this.** Each claim below is marked by how it was checked:
- **[Verified]**: confirmed against the paper's abstract, the publisher's page or the author's own page.
- **[Secondary]**: confirmed only through press coverage or book summaries, so the exact wording may differ from the original.
- **[Unverified]**: could not be confirmed. Do not cite it as fact in the app.

Firecrawl had no credits left, so all searching was done with WebSearch and WebFetch. Full papers behind paywalls were not read; the abstracts and press releases were.

---

## 0. The core finding, in one paragraph

Research keeps finding the same thing. **People think others don't want to talk to them, don't like them, and don't want to go deep, and all three beliefs are wrong.**
- Commuters expected talking to a stranger to be unpleasant. It was pleasant (Epley & Schroeder 2014).
- After a chat, people underestimate how much the other person liked them (Boothby et al. 2018).
- People expect deep talk to be awkward. It is less awkward than they think, and afterwards they prefer it (Kardas, Kumar & Epley 2021).
- People who ask more follow-up questions are liked more (Huang et al. 2017).

So the app's job is not mainly to teach clever lines. It is to correct a wrong belief ("they don't want to hear more from me"), then give him simple, repeatable moves.

## 1. Core principles (13)

### P1. Never answer with one word. Give one real detail.
- **Why it works:** A one-word answer gives the other person nothing to grab. In the ARE method, the "Reveal" step (share something about yourself) is what gives the other person something to answer [Secondary: Fleming via Art of Manliness]. Lorraine K. Lee (CNBC) calls these details "threads": a weekend reply that mentions a hike *and* a niece's birthday gives the listener two things to ask about [Secondary: CNBC].
- **Example:**
  > "How was your weekend?"
  > "Good. I finally tried that new dosa place near the station. The masala dosa was huge."
- **Analogy:** "Good" is a closed door. One detail is a door left open a crack, so the other person can push it.

### P2. End your answer with a hook: hand the ball back.
- **Why it works:** Sprecher et al. (2013) found that pairs who took turns disclosing liked each other more, and felt closer and more similar, than pairs where one person talked and the other only listened [Verified: abstract, JESP 49, 860–866].
- **Example:**
  > "Busy, but good. We shipped a feature I'd been stuck on all week. How about you, a calm day or a crazy one?"
- **Analogy:** Conversation is badminton, not a speech. You hit the shuttle and it should land on their side.

### P3. Ask follow-up questions, not new-topic questions.
- **Why it works:** Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017, JPSP 113, 430–452) found that people who ask more questions are liked more, mostly through follow-ups. A follow-up asks the person to say more about what they just said; a "switch" question jumps to a new topic [Verified: abstract]. Brooks & John (HBR 2018) say follow-ups are "almost always" a good idea and that people do not ask enough of them [Secondary: GovExec summary]. **Caveat:** Kluger & Malloy (2019) reanalysed the speed-dating data. They found the effects were modest and that some of the follow-up effect disappeared under stricter analysis. The original authors replied that their results hold [Verified: PMC6857721 and the PubMed listing of the reply]. The teaching ("ask more follow-ups") is still sound. Just don't promise it will make someone love you.
- **Example:**
  > "I went to Pune this weekend."
  > Weak: "Nice. Did you watch the match?"
  > Strong: "Oh nice, for work or family? What did you do there?"
- **Analogy:** A follow-up is digging deeper in one spot. A switch question is digging a new hole each time. You never reach water.

### P4. People enjoy talking about themselves, so let them.
- **Why it works:** Tamir & Mitchell (2012, PNAS 109(21)) found that 30–40% of what people say is about their own experiences. Talking about yourself activates the brain's reward system, and people gave up money to do it [Verified: abstract. Note the figure is 30–40%, not "40%"]. Carnegie's "six ways to make people like you" include being genuinely interested in others, being a good listener who encourages others to talk about themselves, and talking about the other person's interests [Secondary: multiple summaries of the 1936 book].
- **Example:**
  > "What got you into design in the first place?"
- **Analogy:** Asking about someone's interest is like handing them the mic at karaoke. Most people secretly want it.

### P5. They like you more than you think. Act on that.
- **Why it works:** Boothby, Cooney, Clark & Sandstrom (2018, *Psychological Science* 29(11), 1742–1756) called this the "liking gap." After conversations, people underestimated how much their partner liked them. Among first-year college roommates, the gap lasted for months [Verified: DOI 10.1177/0956797618783714; details Secondary]. One explanation: we are too busy worrying about what we said to notice the other person's signs that they like us.
- **Example (self-talk card):** "That chat felt awkward to me. It probably felt fine to them. Say hi again tomorrow."
- **Analogy:** You hear your own voice on a recording and cringe. Everyone else just hears your normal voice.

### P6. Strangers and acquaintances want to talk more than you assume.
- **Why it works:** Epley & Schroeder (2014, *J. Exp. Psych: General*) found that Chicago train and bus commuters asked to talk to a stranger had a more pleasant ride than those asked to sit alone, even though they predicted the opposite. People assumed others didn't want to talk [Secondary: multiple press reports]. Sandstrom & Dunn (2014) found that a brief friendly chat with a barista (a smile, eye contact, a few words) raised good mood and the feeling of belonging, compared with being purely efficient [Verified: SPPS 5(4), 437–442]. **Caveat:** The participants were mostly urban North Americans.
- **Example:**
  > (Lift, office) "Long day or just getting started?"
- **Analogy:** Everyone at the bus stop is waiting for someone else to speak first. Be the one who goes first.

### P7. Go a little deeper than feels comfortable.
- **Why it works:** Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2021, JPSP) found that people expected deep conversations with strangers to be awkward. They turned out less awkward and more connecting than expected. When people tried both kinds, they preferred the deep one, although they had predicted they would prefer the shallow one. The cause: people underestimate how much others care about what they share [Verified: APA and Chicago Booth summaries of the paper]. Their deep questions included naming what you feel most grateful for, and describing a time you cried in front of someone.
- **Example:**
  > "What's been the best part of your year so far?" (instead of "How's work?")
- **Analogy:** Cold pool water. You think it will be terrible, and after ten seconds you're glad you jumped in.

### P8. Build closeness step by step, and take turns.
- **Why it works:** Aron et al. (1997, PSPB 23(4), 363–377) had strangers ask each other 36 questions in three sets, each more personal than the last. They felt closer than pairs who asked small-talk questions. The method is "sustained, escalating, reciprocal, personal self-disclosure" [Secondary: Greater Good / Berkeley summary of the paper]. Brooks & John (HBR 2018) say that to build a relationship, start with light questions and move to heavier ones. (The reverse order works for getting information, not for building rapport.) [Secondary]
- **Example:** Start with "What do you do for fun?" Weeks later: "What's a moment you'd go back to if you could?"
- **Analogy:** A staircase, not an elevator. Each step should be a small, safe climb.

### P9. Share first, then ask. Don't interrogate.
- **Why it works:** In ARE, you reveal something about yourself *before* the "Encourage" question [Secondary]. Turn-taking disclosure builds liking (Sprecher 2013). FORD guides warn that question after question sounds like a job interview [Secondary: Harvard FAS Career Services and others].
- **Example:**
  > "I was a terrible cook till lockdown. Now I make a decent rajma. Do you cook at all?"
- **Analogy:** A question with nothing shared first is a police interview. A question after you've shared something is an invitation.

### P10. Don't ask so you can talk about yourself ("boomerasking").
- **Why it works:** Brooks & Yeomans named "boomerasking": asking a question only to answer it yourself ("How was your weekend? Mine was amazing, I went to..."). They found the person asking thinks they come across well, while the listener finds it self-centred [Secondary: Imperial College repository + press. The year is unclear, either 2025 or 2026].
- **Example:**
  > Bad: "How was your weekend? Mine was crazy, I went to Goa..."
  > Good: "How was your weekend?" → listen → follow up → *then* share Goa if they ask.
- **Analogy:** It's like inviting a friend to dinner and then eating their plate too.

### P11. Show you heard them: repeat, name the feeling, check.
- **Why it works:** Duhigg's "looping for understanding": ask, repeat what you heard in your own words, then check you got it right [Verified: Duhigg's TIME essay]. Chris Voss's *mirroring* (repeat their last one to three key words, curiously, then pause) and *labelling* ("It sounds like…", "It seems like…") come from hostage negotiation [Secondary: book summaries]. Leil Lowndes describes a similar "parroting" move. A critic warns that by the third echo you sound fake [Secondary].
- **Example:**
  > "It's been a mad week with the audit."
  > "The audit? Sounds like it's eating your whole week."
- **Analogy:** It's like a waiter reading your order back to you. You instantly feel looked after.

### P12. Match the kind of conversation the other person is having.
- **Why it works:** Duhigg calls this the "matching principle": people connect when they're in the same mode. He says matching matters most when someone gets emotional, when the talk turns practical, or when it goes somewhere unexpected [Verified: TIME essay]. Most laughter is a response to something ordinary, and laughing back signals you want to connect [Verified: TIME essay].
- **Example:**
  > Colleague, tired: "Ugh, Mondays." → Match: "Tell me about it. Chai first, then emails?" Don't reply with "Actually, I love Mondays!"
- **Analogy:** Dancing. If they're doing a slow song, you don't start doing bhangra.

### P13. End before you run out. Exit warmly, on purpose.
- **Why it works:** Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson (2021, PNAS) studied 932 conversations. Conversations almost never ended when both people wanted them to. The average gap between wanted and actual length was about half the conversation, and people couldn't tell when their partner wanted to stop [Verified: abstract]. Since nobody can read the other's mind, a clear, kind exit helps both people.
- **Example:**
  > "I've got a call at 3, but this was really fun. Tell me how the Pune trip goes!"
- **Analogy:** It's like leaving a party while the music's still good. People remember the high point.

## 2. Named techniques (with two-line dialogues)

**ARE: Anchor, Reveal, Encourage** (Carol Fleming, *It's the Way You Say It*) [Secondary]
- **Anchor:** a comment on something you both see or share. **Reveal:** a small detail about you. **Encourage:** a question.
> (In the canteen) "This queue is longer than our sprint backlog. I'm here only for the samosas, honestly. What do you usually get?"
> "Ha, the pav bhaji. Thursday is pav bhaji day."

**FORD: Family, Occupation, Recreation, Dreams** (origin unknown; widely taught, e.g. by Harvard FAS Career Services) [Secondary]
- Four topic buckets to draw questions from. With strangers at work, start with O and R. Family and Dreams come only after you've built some rapport.
> "What do you get up to on weekends, any hobbies?"
> "I've started cycling, actually." → "Oh nice, how far do you go?"

**Follow-up question** (Huang et al. 2017) [Verified]
> "I just moved from Bangalore."
> "Oh, what made you move? Do you miss it?"

**Free information / threads** (Garner, *Conversationally Speaking*, ch. 4 "Taking Advantage of Free Information" [the chapter title is verified, its content is not]; "threads" per Lorraine K. Lee, CNBC [Secondary])
- Notice the extra details people drop and ask about them. Drop some yourself.
> "Weekend was good, I was at my cousin's engagement in Jaipur."
> "Jaipur! Was it a big function? Did you get to eat anything good?"

**Threading with "Speaking of…"** (Craig Gibbons, *We Should Be Social* / Jaunty) [Secondary]
- Take one word from what they said and add your own related story. No question needed.
> "We watched a movie with the family."
> "Speaking of movies, I finally saw *12th Fail*. Have you seen it?"

**Mirroring** (Chris Voss) [Secondary]
- Repeat their last one to three key words with a curious tone, then stay quiet.
> "The client changed the whole brief again."
> "The whole brief?" (pause; they explain more)

**Labelling** (Chris Voss) [Secondary]
- Name the feeling you notice: "It sounds like… / It seems like…". Avoid "I understand."
> "Third late night this week."
> "Sounds like this launch is really draining you."

**Looping for understanding** (Duhigg) [Verified]
> "So the problem isn't the deadline, it's that nobody told you about it. Did I get that right?"
> "Exactly!"

**Callback** (comedy and conversation-coaching term; no research source found [Unverified as a named method])
- Later in the chat, or days later, refer back to something they said earlier.
> (Monday) "Did your daughter's dance recital go well?"
> "You remembered! Yes, she was brilliant."

**Graceful exit** (built on Mastroianni 2021 [Verified finding]; the scripts are ours)
- Give a reason, say something warm, and leave the door open for next time.
> "I'll let you get back to it. Really enjoyed hearing about the trek."
> "Same here, catch you at lunch."

**Escalating questions** (Aron 1997 [Secondary]; Kardas 2021 [Verified])
> "What's something you're looking forward to this year?" → later → "What's a moment you'd go back to if you could?"

## 3. Layered curriculum

### Level 1: Answer well (never "fine")
1. **"Fine" closes the door.** Rule: a one-word answer gives them nothing to say back. Swap it for one detail.
2. **The 1+1 formula.** Rule: a feeling word plus one detail. "Tiring, but good. We closed the vendor deal."
3. **Use specific nouns.** Rule: names, places and food beat adjectives. "Good" → "Watched the India match at a friend's place."
4. **The Hook Return.** Rule: end your answer with "…and you?" or a more specific question back.
5. **Honest but light.** Rule: if your day was bad, you can say so with a small smile. "Rough one, my laptop died twice. Yours better?"
6. **Read the "drive-by" hello.** Rule: some "How are you?" questions are only a greeting, like in a corridor. Reply with one line and a smile (Debra Fine suggests one sentence when the asker may not mean it [Secondary: RNZ interview]).
7. **Keep a "fresh detail" ready.** Rule: each morning, think of one interesting thing from yesterday (food, show, news, small win) to use as your answer.
8. **Plain English beats fancy English.** Rule: short, clear sentences sound confident. Grammar slips matter less than a dead answer.

### Level 2: Keep it going
1. **Pick up their thread.** Rule: listen for the extra detail they dropped and ask about it.
2. **Alternate comments and questions.** Rule: comment, question, comment, so it never feels like an interrogation (Art of Manliness on ARE).
3. **"Speaking of…"** Rule: take one word they said and add your own small story.
4. **Mirror one word.** Rule: repeat their key word as a question, then pause.
5. **Silence is OK.** Rule: wait two seconds after they finish. They often say more.
6. **Match their energy.** Rule: tired person → calm reply. Excited person → excited reply (Duhigg's matching).
7. **Don't top their story.** Rule: "Oh, that's nothing, I once…" kills it. Ask one more question first. (This matches Headlee's "don't equate your experience with theirs" [Secondary: summary of TED talk].)
8. **Rescue a dying chat.** Rule: go back to an earlier thread: "Wait, you said you went to Jaipur?"

### Level 3: Ask good questions
1. **Open vs closed.** Rule: "Did you like it?" gets a yes or no. "What did you like about it?" gets a story (Headlee; Fine).
2. **Swap the stale question.** Rule: "How was your weekend?" → "What was the highlight of your weekend?" (CNBC / Lorraine Lee).
3. **The follow-up habit.** Rule: ask at least two follow-ups before changing topic (Huang 2017).
4. **ARE opener for strangers.** Rule: shared thing → a bit about you → a question.
5. **FORD buckets.** Rule: if your mind goes blank, think Occupation → Recreation → (later) Family → Dreams.
6. **"How did you get into…?"** Rule: origin questions invite stories (Duhigg's lawyer example).
7. **Don't boomerask.** Rule: if you ask, really listen to their answer before sharing yours.
8. **"What's that like?"** Rule: an all-purpose follow-up that turns facts into feelings (Fine's "what was that like for you" [Secondary: VOA]).
9. **Loop it back.** Rule: "So you're saying… right?" shows you understood.

### Level 4: Go deeper safely
1. **They want depth more than you think.** Rule: people underestimate how much others care (Kardas 2021). Try one deeper question per chat.
2. **The staircase.** Rule: light → medium → deep. Never jump straight to the top (Aron 1997).
3. **Go first.** Rule: before asking a deep question, share a small honest answer of your own. It makes it safe for them.
4. **"Why" questions, gently.** Rule: ask about values and choices: "What made you pick product over engineering?"
5. **Read the signals.** Rule: short answers, looking away, or changing the topic mean step back down a level. No offence taken.
6. **Name the feeling.** Rule: "That sounds like it meant a lot to you" (Voss's labelling).
7. **Offer an easy way out.** Rule: "You don't have to answer, but I'm curious…" keeps deep questions safe.
8. **Office vs friend depth.** Rule: at work, go deep on work stories and passions, not family problems or money.
9. **Hold what they share carefully.** Rule: never repeat a personal thing someone told you to others.

### Level 5: Exits, group talk, follow-ups days later
1. **Exit before it fizzles.** Rule: leave on a high point (Mastroianni 2021).
2. **The three-part exit.** Rule: reason + warm line + future hook.
3. **Joining a group.** Rule: stand at the edge, listen, nod, add something to *their* current topic before starting a new one.
4. **Include the quiet one.** Rule: "Riya, you've been to Ladakh, right?" Pulling someone in makes you look like a leader.
5. **The callback, days later.** Rule: note one detail, then ask about it next time. "How did the interview go?"
6. **Remember names.** Rule: say their name once, early (Carnegie: a person's name is to them the sweetest sound [Secondary]).
7. **The "liking gap" reset.** Rule: after an awkward chat, assume it went better than you think, and say hi again (Boothby 2018).
8. **Follow up by message.** Rule: "Saw this and thought of your cycling trip" plus a link keeps the connection going.
9. **Small talk with your manager.** Rule: brief, warm, about work interests or weekends. Take your cue from how much they share.

## 4. Question ladders (light → medium → deep)

Key: **O** = fine in the office or with a new acquaintance; **A** = acquaintance you've met a few times; **F** = friend or close colleague only.

### Light (safe almost anywhere)
1. What was the highlight of your weekend? (O)
2. Any plans for the long weekend? (O)
3. What's keeping you busy this week? (O)
4. Best thing you ate recently? (O; food is the safest topic in India)
5. Watching anything good these days? (O)
6. How's the new project shaping up? (O)
7. How do you usually get to office? Long commute? (O)
8. Tea or coffee person? (O)
9. Which part of the city do you stay in? How do you like it? (O)
10. How did you celebrate Diwali / Eid / Christmas / Onam? (O; ask about the festival they mention, don't assume their religion)
11. Done any travelling lately? (O)

### Medium (some rapport needed)
12. How did you get into this field? (O/A)
13. What do you enjoy most about your work? (O/A)
14. What's something you're looking forward to this year? (A)
15. What do you do when you want to fully switch off? (A)
16. Is there a skill you're learning or want to learn? (A)
17. Where did you grow up? What was it like? (A; Duhigg's small-town example)
18. Best trip you've ever taken? (A)
19. What's a book, show or podcast that changed how you think? (A)
20. If you weren't doing this job, what would you be doing? (A)
21. What's the best advice you've ever got? (A)
22. What's something you're proud of that people don't know? (A/F)

### Deep (friend, or a long one-to-one chat where they've opened up)
23. What's your favourite childhood memory? (F, or A if the mood is warm)
24. If you could go back to one moment in your life, which would it be? (F)
25. Who has influenced you the most? (A/F)
26. What are you most grateful for right now? (F; used in Kardas 2021)
27. What did you believe at 20 that you don't believe now? (F)
28. What's something you wish you'd done differently? (F; regrets, so only with trust)
29. What does a really good day look like for you? (A/F)
30. What would you do if you knew you couldn't fail? (F)
31. When did you last feel really proud of yourself? (F)
32. What's a moment that changed the direction of your life? (F)
33. What do you want people to remember about you? (F)

**Cultural caution: Indian and office contexts** [Secondary: business-etiquette guides, not research]
- **Avoid at work:** salary and money ("How much do you earn?"), caste, religion as a debate topic, politics, and marriage pressure ("When are you getting married?" or "Why no kids yet?"). Also avoid divorce, health problems, and comments on weight or skin colour.
- **Safe and warm:** food, festivals (ask about how *they* celebrate), cricket and films (if they're interested), travel, hometown, commute, weekend plans.
- **Hierarchy:** with seniors, keep it lighter and shorter, and let them set how deep it goes. Questions about family are common in India, but in mixed or international teams they can feel intrusive. Let the other person bring up family first.
- **Hometown questions:** "Where are you from?" is normal in India. But don't use someone's state or surname to guess their caste or community, and never comment on it.
- **Deep questions at work:** keep them about the person's life, not their private struggles. "What's a moment you'd go back to?" is fine over a long lunch with a colleague you know. It's not fine in a two-minute lift ride.
- **The research above mostly used US and UK participants.** The *direction* (people underestimate others' interest) is probably general, but how deep is "appropriate" varies by culture. Teach him to watch the other person's signals.

23. "What are you up to this evening?" — "Nothing." → "Probably cooking and a call with my parents. Something fun on your side?"
24. (Ending) — just walking away → "I'll let you go. Really enjoyed this. Tell me how the interview goes!"

## 6. Rules the AI must follow when generating small-talk cards

**Do**
1. Every card has: **one rule** in one plain sentence (at most 15 words), **one or two example exchanges** (two to four lines each), and **one everyday analogy** (from cricket, chai, traffic, cooking or office life).
2. Base every rule on a principle in Sections 1–2. If a card cites research, use only sources marked [Verified] or [Secondary] in Section 7, and never make up numbers.
3. Use **Indian office settings and names** (Priya, Rahul, Riya, Arjun, Fatima, Joseph), Indian food and places, and festivals from different communities, with variety.
4. Write in **plain, natural spoken English**: contractions, short sentences, nothing like "I am doing well, thank you for asking."
5. Show **both sides** in examples: what he says and what a realistic person might reply.
6. "Better" answers must include **one concrete detail plus a hook** (a question or an obvious thread).
7. Label how deep each question is (light, medium, deep) and where it fits (office, acquaintance, friend).
8. Build skills in order: a Level 4 card must not assume he can't yet do Level 1.
9. Include **"signals to step back"** on every Level 4 card.
10. Keep examples **true-to-life and modest**: no bragging, no pickup-artist tactics, no manipulation.

**Don't**
1. Don't teach scripts that sound fake or salesy ("Wow, that's fascinating, tell me more!" on every turn).
2. Don't include salary, caste, religion debates, politics, weight or appearance, or marriage and kids pressure in office examples.
3. Don't use boomerasking, story-topping or rapid-fire interview questions in a "good" example.
4. Don't present Voss or Lowndes tricks as science. They come from practitioners. Say "a negotiation expert suggests…"
5. Don't overclaim: "asking follow-ups makes people like you more" is fine; "guarantees" or "makes them fall in love" is not (the Aron study measured closeness, not love; Huang's effects were modest).
6. Don't use the unverified names "Never the Naked City" or "word detective" (Lowndes), or a "36 questions = love" claim.
7. Don't use idioms he may not know without explaining them ("break the ice" → explain: start a conversation with someone new).
8. Don't make "good" answers too long. Two sentences is the target, three at most.

**Quality bar (check each card against all five)**
- Would a real Indian office worker actually say this out loud? (natural)
- Does the "better" reply give the other person at least one clear thing to ask about? (a hook)
- Is the rule correct and traceable to a source in Section 7? (true)
- Is the depth right for the stated setting? (safe)
- Can he use it tomorrow? (usable)

This ties in with the project's existing "AI content quality gate" memory: generated cards must pass a check before they are shown to him.
