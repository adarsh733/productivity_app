How every card teaches

The same shape, every time

  1. Colour + level + kind: you always know where you are (💬 Small talk · L1 · Learn).
  2. One idea only: a rule in one plain sentence.
  3. Two examples: one at work, one in everyday life. You need to see it used.
  4. "Instead of": the weak version you'd normally say, when there is one.
  5. An everyday comparison: "conversation is badminton, not a speech".
  6. A recall moment: think of the answer before you see it. Pulling it from memory is what makes it stick.
  7. Save ⭐ and Again ↻: saved cards go to Me. "Again" brings the card back sooner.
Why this shape?

Seeing a word 10 times is enough to recognise it, but not to use it. Recalling and making your own sentence is what moves it into your speech. Cards come back at growing gaps (1, 3, 7, 16, 35 days), which feels harder but works better.

Webb 2007 · Roediger & Karpicke 2006 · Cepeda et al. 2008 · Kornell 2009. The 1-3-7-16-35 schedule is our choice, inspired by the research.

The research, in plain words

What the books and studies agree on

I went through more than 100 sources: studies, books (Storyworthy, Supercommunicators, The Fine Art of Small Talk, The Pyramid Principle, Think Faster Talk Smarter, Made to Stick) and expert guides. These ideas became the AI's rulebook.

💬 People want to talk to you more than you think.

People expect chatting with a stranger to be unpleasant, and it isn't. After a chat, the other person liked you more than you guess. Deep talk is less awkward than expected, and people prefer it afterwards. The first lesson fixes this belief.

Epley & Schroeder 2014 · Boothby et al. 2018 · Kardas, Kumar & Epley 2021

💬 Follow-up questions make people like you more.

Ask more about what they just said, not a new topic. Share a little first, then ask, so it never feels like an interview. Don't ask a question just to talk about yourself.

Huang et al. 2017 (modest effect) · Sprecher et al. 2013 · Brooks & Yeomans

📖 A story is a change, not a list.

Replace "and then… and then…" with "but" and "so". Every story needs a moment where something changes. Start in the action and know your last line before you begin.

Matthew Dicks, Storyworthy · Randy Olson, And-But-Therefore · The Moth

📖 Small details beat big words.

"My Activa died at 9:58" beats "I had a vehicle problem in the morning". Name the person, the place, the time. Add one line of real dialogue.

Heath & Heath, Made to Stick · Green & Brock 2000

🎯 Say the point first.

Four ready shapes cover nearly every answer: Point-Reason-Example-Point; What / So what / Now what; Situation-Problem-Answer; Bottom line first. With a shape, you're never short of words.

Toastmasters (PREP) · Matt Abrahams · Barbara Minto

🔤 Learn phrases, not just words, and in both directions.

"Meet a deadline" sticks better than "deadline". Practise from meaning to English, because that's the direction speaking goes. "Um" is normal. Replace it with "let me think", don't ban it.

Boers et al. 2006 · Laufer & Hulstijn 2001 · Clark & Fox Tree 2002

Left out on purpose: "stories release oxytocin" (didn't hold up when others re-tested it) and "36 questions make you fall in love" (an overstatement). The AI is told never to claim these.

The AI rulebook

One rulebook the AI follows every time

Whenever the AI makes cards, answers an Ask, or gives chat feedback, it reads this rulebook first. A second AI then checks each new card against the same rules before you see it.

Every card

  • One idea, plain words
  • A work example and an everyday example
  • An everyday comparison and a recall moment
  • Useful everyday words, never rare or showy ones
  • Slips explained as "in global English…"
  • No made-up idioms, no fake science

Every day

  • 3–5 new items a day, the rest reviews
  • Mixes all 4 skills, leaning on what you ask about
  • Your weak spots come back until they're fixed
  • No repeats, no near-duplicates
  • Office-safe topics: food, travel, festivals. Never salary, caste or marriage

Every chat answer

  • Teach first, judge second
  • At most 3 points: 1 thing you did well, 1–2 fixes
  • Always a better version you can say again
  • Speed and loudness only from your phone's measurement
  • Each fix links to the lesson that teaches it
